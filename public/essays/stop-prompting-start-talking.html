A lot of the sharpest thinking I’ve done this year has happened in conversation with an AI. It asked me questions I hadn’t thought to ask myself.

That claim will bother people who care about clear thinking, because the reigning advice is that writing is thinking. Paul Graham put it well: “If you never subject your ideas to that test, you’ll not only never have fully formed ideas, but also never realize it.” He’s right. Writing is thinking. But it’s only one of two ways to refine your thinking. The other is conversation, and conversation turns out to be the more fundamental one.

Vygotsky figured this out in the 1930s. Children think by talking to others before they learn to think silently. The developmental sequence goes: social speech, then private speech, then inner speech. That voice in your head, the one you think of as “thinking,” is conversation that has been folded inward. Writing reverses the process: it takes inner speech and externalizes it so you can examine it. But conversation came first. Thought is internalized dialogue.

This matters because it reframes what AI is actually good for. The “don’t use AI to write” crowd is protecting the first mechanism — the solitary refinement of putting your ideas in sequence. They’re right that prompting AI to “write me a strategy memo” skips that process. An NBER study of 1.5 million ChatGPT conversations found that nearly 78% are exactly this: practical guidance, writing tasks, information retrieval. Prompt in, output out. You got text. You skipped the thinking.

But these critics treat writing as the only thinking mechanism worth protecting. It’s like watching someone use a piano as a table and concluding pianos are bad furniture. AI can do something no writing tool ever could: hold the other side of a conversation.

I used to think Joan Didion was posturing when she wrote “I write entirely to find out what I’m thinking.” Then I started writing seriously and realized she was being precise. You sit down knowing roughly what you believe. By the third paragraph, you’ve found a contradiction. By the fifth, you’ve abandoned your original thesis. Writing forces serialization: in your head, ideas coexist as a comfortable cloud of contradiction, and the moment you put them in sequence, the contradictions announce themselves.

Conversation does something different. It forces *response*. A blank page waits for you. An interlocutor doesn’t.

There’s a tradition in programming called rubber duck debugging: you explain your code to a rubber duck on your desk, and the act of articulating forces you to translate implicit mental models into explicit statements. The duck doesn’t need expertise or consciousness. The mechanism is in the explaining. But the duck can’t ask: “What happens when the input is null?”

That question is the whole difference. Being questioned forces you to produce an answer, not just recognize one (what learning scientists call the generation effect), and retrieving what you know strengthens the knowing (the testing effect). These are two of the best-documented phenomena in cognitive science. A conversation partner who asks genuine questions isn’t just being helpful. They’re triggering the machinery that produces the deepest thinking.

So what happens when you say to Claude: “I have a half-formed idea. Ask me questions until it’s sharp”? The AI asks: “What specifically is different about your claim versus what people already believe?” You pause. You realize you’ve been assuming your idea is novel without ever articulating what it displaces. You try to answer. It’s clumsy. The AI follows up. Your second answer is better, because the first attempt revealed where your reasoning was thin.

This isn’t a nice-to-have feature. A study in Science found that personalized AI dialogue reduced conspiracy beliefs by roughly 20%, with effects lasting over two months. The AI engaged each person’s specific reasoning and asked questions tailored to their evidence. Dialogue changed minds. Output did not.

A meta-analysis of 106 experiments on human-AI collaboration found something complementary: human-AI systems performed *worse* than either alone on decision tasks, but trended positive on creative ones. Delegating to AI makes you worse. Conversing with it makes you better. How you use AI matters more than which AI you use.

There is a serious counterargument, and the essay falls apart without it.

A 2026 study in Science measured the gap directly: across eleven major language models, AI affirms users roughly 49% more often than humans do. Even when human evaluators judged users as wrong, AI still affirmed them about half the time. Users who got sycophantic responses became more confident in wrong positions and less willing to reconsider. Worse: they *preferred* the agreeable models, rating them as higher quality.

The value of conversation comes from friction. A human interlocutor has their own perspective, their own stakes. A Socratic partner that agrees with everything you say is a mirror that flatters, not a midwife that delivers.

I don’t think this objection is fully resolved. AI systems are trained on human preference signals, and humans prefer agreement. The incentive gradient runs against honest questioning. But the capability exists independently of how it is currently deployed. Some models, some of the time, do push back on weak reasoning, do hold up a thought long enough to examine it. The capability is here. The culture of use is not.

And even imperfect dialectic produces something the prompt-in-output-out model cannot: the experience of hearing “Why do you believe that?” and realizing you don’t have a good answer. A sycophantic interlocutor is worse than a good human one. It is still better than no interlocutor at all, which is what most knowledge workers have at 2 AM when the idea won’t let them sleep.

Socrates distrusted writing. In the Phaedrus, he argued that written words “cannot defend themselves by argument” and “cannot teach the truth effectually.” They sit on the page, inert, unable to respond to questions. For 2,400 years, he was right. Every book ever written could not ask a single question back.

Now something can.

Use AI to write. Use it the way Plato used Socrates: not to produce text, but to produce thinking. Let it question your assumptions. Let the conversation take you somewhere you didn’t expect. Then write. If what you end up writing is basically what you sat down to say, you used AI as a typewriter. If the conversation moved you somewhere new, you used it as a thinking tool. The test is not whether AI touched the words, but whether the thinking moved.
